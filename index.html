Your name on a Japanese booking form

KireiPass and most clinic forms ask for 姓 (surname), 名 (given name) and フリガナ (a katakana reading).

Type your name in Latin letters exactly as printed in your passport. Staff match the booking to your passport at reception, and the alphabet spelling is what they check.

  • The katakana field (フリガナ) is a reading, not a translation. If you already have a katakana reading, paste it; otherwise leave it blank or write the same spelling in Latin letters.
  • Do not invent a Japanese name to make the form accept it — a mismatch with your passport causes problems at reception and at customs.
  • Some forms ask for a Japanese address and phone number. See the address tip below; a foreign mobile number is usually accepted in 3-4-4 format with the country code.
  • Bring your passport on the day. The booking, the prescription and the receipt should all carry the same name.

Booking-form tips

  • Most booking forms are Japanese only. Turn on your browser's translate feature and keep the name spelling identical to your passport.
  • A foreign mobile number usually fits the 3-4-4 split; add your country code where a form asks for one.
  • Use the address of the hotel you will actually stay at. Only use 0000000 when the hotel is not booked yet and the form accepts it — then tell the clinic the real address once you have it.
  • If stock runs short, a clinic may ship the pens later within Japan, so keep your hotel address, check-in date and the name on the booking handy.
  • No confirmation email may mean no booking. Check the spam folder before travelling.
  • For two or more people, most clinics want one booking per person with a different email and phone number.

What this total does and does not include

  • Every clinic price here is self-pay (outside Japanese health insurance) and includes Japanese consumption tax.
  • Japan tax-free does not apply to a self-pay clinic prescription. A prescription is not a tax-free-shop purchase, so the pens cannot be bought duty-free at the clinic.
  • Check your home country's personal-import and customs rules for prescription medicines, including GLP-1 pens, before you travel. Limits, paperwork and permitted quantities differ by country — this app only covers the Japanese side.
  • A first-visit fee, an English prescription, hotel delivery or a cold bag may be charged on top; add them under Other.
  • Figures are a preparation aid. The clinic decides the final charge and the doctor decides whether and at what dose a prescription is issued.

보냉 실무 메모

  • 보냉제는 얼린 상태여도 젤·액체류로 분류됩니다. 개당 100g(100mL) 이하만 1L 투명 지퍼백(1인 1개)에 담아 반입할 수 있습니다.
  • 일본 다이소 보냉제는 80g×3, 50g×6처럼 절취선으로 붙은 제품이 많습니다. 가위로 낱개 분리해야 개당 100g 이하로 인정되기 쉽습니다(처음부터 분리된 70g×2 제품도 있음).
  • 냉장 의약품과 동봉한 보냉제는 용량 예외가 인정되지만, 보안검색대에서 꺼내 위 문구와 함께 보여주는 편이 수월합니다.
  • 얼린 팩이 펜에 직접 닿으면 동결될 수 있습니다. 수건·키친타월로 한 겹 감싸고, 반드시 기내 수하물로 들고 타세요(위탁 화물칸은 동결 위험).
  • 일본 현지 기준이며, 항공사·공항에 따라 운영이 다를 수 있습니다. 출국 전 항공사 안내를 한 번 더 확인하세요.

Cold-chain practice at the airport

  • Ice and gel packs count as gel or liquid even when frozen. Security allows pieces of 100 g (100 mL) or less, together in one 1 L clear zip bag per person.
  • Japanese 100-yen-shop packs often come joined by cut-lines (80 g × 3, 50 g × 6). Separate them with scissors so each piece is under 100 g; some packs (70 g × 2) are already split.
  • Packs packed with refrigerated medicine are normally given an allowance, but take them out and show the Japanese phrase above with your documents.
  • A frozen pack touching a pen can freeze it. Wrap the packs in a towel or kitchen paper and keep them in your cabin bag — the cargo hold can freeze them.
  • This is Japanese practice as published; airlines and airports differ. Check your airline's rules once more before departure.
